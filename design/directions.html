Direction A

The Receipt

Map first: a big county map shaded by dollars back, with a search box. Each county page is an itemized receipt: line items for the money in, taxes as the "payment", and dollars back per $1 as the bottom line. The receipt makes a page you can grasp in two seconds and screenshot.

  • UK Annual Tax Summary: the government sends each taxpayer a statement of where their tax went.
  • Receiptify: listening history printed as a receipt; it shows how well the format spreads on social media.
  • Thermal-printer type: monospace, dotted leaders, a torn edge. IBM Plex Mono and Public Sans.
Page by page

Home

Full-width map, search by county or ZIP, and a strip of "biggest changes this year" (after Phase 1). Hovering a county shows a mini receipt.

County page

Receipt grouped into benefits, grants and contracts, then taxes and the bottom line. Below it, a second receipt of the top recipients, and a "history" printout with one line per year.

Map

One ochre-to-umber scale on cream paper, in equal-sized groups. State lines in umber.

Type & color

IBM Plex Mono for figures, Public Sans for text. Cream paper, umber ink, one ochre accent.

Motion

On first visit the receipt "prints" line by line and the total ticks up. Turned off for people who prefer reduced motion.

On phones

The best fit of all seven: a receipt is already narrow.

Good at

Instant understanding and sharing. A "save receipt as image" button is easy to add.

Watch out for

"Receipt" implies a purchase ("you paid, you got"), which oversimplifies taxes. Long tables in monospace get tiring. Looks a lot like your Rx Receipt site.

Build effort

Low–medium.

Direction B

The Almanac

A reference book. A quiet map with a state list beside it. County pages are fact sheets: two headline numbers, clean tables, a numbered source note on every figure, and a download button on every table. Closest to the "plain civic reference" tone you picked.

  • Our World in Data: every chart has sources, a download button and a written method.
  • Census QuickFacts: a one-page fact sheet for any county.
  • USAFacts: neutral government-finance explainers.
Page by page

Home

Map plus search, a "browse by state" list and a short "how to read this" paragraph. The methods page is linked prominently.

County page

Headline figures, a money-in / taxes table, 5-year sparklines per category, a top-recipients table, a "statewide programs (estimated)" section, footnotes, and CSV downloads.

Map

Gray-to-teal, white state lines. A "show as table" switch for screen readers and for people who don't read maps.

Type & color

Source Serif 4 headings; Public Sans text (the font of the U.S. Web Design System). White page, teal accent.

Motion

Almost none: gentle fades. Nothing counts up.

On phones

Tables turn into stacked rows; the map can be pinch-zoomed.

Good at

Trust, neutrality and citations. Lots of plain text helps search engines. The easiest to keep accurate.

Watch out for

Can feel dry and is the least shareable, so it needs a good share image (see the mixes below).

Build effort

Low.

Direction C

The Ledger

Money in, money out. The map uses a two-sided scale centered on the typical county, so a place reads as above or below the middle. County pages show two columns of bars, what came in and what residents paid. A later version could turn the bars into a flow from each program into the county.

  • USAspending Spending Explorer: drill from a budget function down to a program.
  • Open Knowledge Foundation's Where Does My Money Go? / OpenSpending (2009–2011, now offline): UK spending explored by region and type.
  • FT Visual Vocabulary: when a two-sided scale fits.
Page by page

Home

Two-sided map with a legend that explains the middle. Headline: "above or below the typical county?"

County page

Came in vs. paid out, side by side, with a balance line. Later a flow diagram from programs into the county and out to tax types.

Map

Brown-to-teal, centered on the median county ($), not the U.S. average, because most counties sit above the average.

Type & color

IBM Plex Sans and Mono. Warm gray page; teal for money in, brown for taxes.

Motion

Bars grow in; later, particles moving along the flow lines.

On phones

The two columns stack: in, then out.

Good at

Showing both sides at once; comparisons are obvious.

Watch out for

Two-sided maps invite a "winners and losers" reading, which makes this the most partisan-risky option. Wording like "balance" must never become "owed".

Build effort

Medium (the flow diagram is extra).

Direction D · new

The Atlas

A printed national atlas, with one big idea: a Land / People switch. On a normal map, huge thinly populated western counties take most of the ink, while most Americans live in small, dense counties. The People view draws each county as a circle sized by population, so the map is weighted by people. Try the switch on the mockup.

  • National Atlas of the United States (USGS, 1970–2014): the government's own reference atlas.
  • swisstopo: hairline detail, condensed labels, calm earth colors.
  • Danny Dorling's population cartograms (Worldmapper): circles sized by people.
Page by page

Home

Hero map with the Land / People switch, search, and click-to-zoom into a state. The switch remembers your choice.

County page

An atlas "plate": a locator map, a strip of big numbers, the county's place among its neighbors ("vs. the counties next door"), and the state drawn as a small people-weighted map.

Map

Sage-to-forest scale, hairline gray borders, labels in condensed capitals like a printed atlas.

Type & color

Barlow Condensed for headings and labels, Barlow for text. Off-white paper, charcoal ink, greens.

Motion

Circles slide out from each county's center when switching to People, and back again.

On phones

The People view reads well on a small screen; the Land view needs zooming.

Good at

Fixes the biggest honesty problem of county maps (land isn't people). Beautiful, and map lovers share it.

Watch out for

Population cartograms are unfamiliar, so they need a one-line explainer. More code, and a heavier first page load.

Build effort

Medium–high.

Direction E · new

The County Card

Every county is a collectible card. The front has the county's shape, the big number and its rank; the back has a stat table like the back of a baseball card. The full details sit in plain tables below the card. Cards make it natural to compare two places, share an image, or use the site in a classroom.

  • Topps baseball card backs: a dense, much-loved stat table in a small space.
  • Passport to Your National Parks stamps: collecting the places you've been.
  • Wikipedia infoboxes: the same key facts in the same spot on every page.
Page by page

Home

"Deal me a county" (random card), search, and "compare two cards". Optional: collect counties you've lived in or visited, saved in your browser, no accounts.

County page

Card front and back (tap to flip), "save card as image", then the full tables, trend and recipients in a plain layout.

Map

A smaller map on the home page; the card is the hook. Each card's color comes from its census region (four earth tones), not its result.

Type & color

Archivo, a variable font that can go wide and bold for names or narrow for stat tables. Cream card stock.

Motion

Card flip; a short "deal" animation; cards fan out in compare mode.

On phones

One card fills the screen; swipe to move to neighboring counties.

Good at

The most shareable, and works for teachers and local news. Comparing places feels natural.

Watch out for

A playful tone can trivialize the subject, and a rank on a card invites competition. Keep the rank quiet ("of 3,144") and the method one tap away.

Build effort

Medium (share images need a small image-making step at build time).

Direction F · new

The Statement

A year-end account statement for each county. Sections for benefits, grants, contracts and taxes, each with columns for this year, the 5-year average and the change. An "adjustments" section lists cancellations and money taken back, with dates. Built around the trend and cancellation features, and it prints cleanly for a council meeting or a newsroom.

  • Year-end bank statements: summary box first, then itemized sections.
  • Pay stubs: "current" and "year-to-date" columns side by side, here "this year" and "5-year average".
  • The annual Social Security Statement's plain layout, borrowed for layout only (no seals or official look).
Page by page

Home

"Find your county's statement": search plus an index of states with ratio bars. A small grayscale map.

County page

Header with "period covered" and "data as of". Summary box, sections with this year / 5-year average / change, the adjustments list, and a printable footer with sources.

Map

Home page only, small and gray, so the statement stays the focus.

Type & color

Libre Franklin text, DM Mono figures. White, charcoal, one ink-green accent.

Motion

None beyond hover highlights. A print stylesheet makes a one-page PDF.

On phones

Shows "this year" by default, with a switch for the other columns.

Good at

Change over time and cancellations, the features no competitor does at county level. Printable.

Watch out for

Must never look like an official government document (no seals, no "Department of"). Dense. It's another document style, close to Rx Receipt's lab-report look.

Build effort

Low–medium.

Direction G · new

The Explorer

The home page is the tool: a map that fills the screen, a side panel for the selected county, chips to switch the measure (dollars back, money per person, Social Security share, contracts share), and a compare mode that pins up to three counties. Every view has its own link, so any comparison can be shared.

  • The Opportunity Atlas (Census Bureau + Opportunity Insights): a neutral, map-first explorer of local data.
  • Census Bureau My Community Explorer (2022): county outlines with a data panel, zooming down to tracts.
  • Map apps (Google Maps, Zillow): map plus panel on desktop, bottom sheet on phones.
Page by page

Home

The app itself: map, measure chips, search and compare. There's also a plain page per county for search engines and sharing.

County page

The panel, expanded: key figures, the county's rank in its state, neighbors, trend and recipients.

Map

Sand-to-deep-green; the selected county is outlined; zoom and pan; the colors change with the chosen measure.

Type & color

Figtree, a friendly app font. Light gray background, white panels, deep green accent.

Motion

The map glides to the selected county; the panel slides in.

On phones

Map on top, details in a bottom sheet you drag up, like a map app.

Good at

Journalists and researchers; many measures without new pages; strong for comparisons.

Watch out for

The heaviest to build and test. It can overwhelm casual visitors, and the plain county pages are still needed.

Build effort

High.

Mix & match

The directions combine well. Three combinations worth considering:

Almanac + Atlas

B's fact-sheet county pages, with D's Land / People map on the home page. The most defensible pairing, and it fixes the map's land-vs-people bias. My pick if you want one.

Almanac + Card

B's pages for reading, plus an E-style card image for sharing: every page gets a "share this county" card. Trust for readers, reach for social media.

Statement + Explorer

F's statements for each county, with G's explorer as the home page. Best if journalists and local officials are the main audience. The most work.

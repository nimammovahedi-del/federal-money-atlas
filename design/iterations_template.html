Putting the answer at the center

Three iterations on the adopted design (Atlas + Ledger + Statement + Explorer). Each keeps its look and data but rebuilds the home and county pages around one question: for every $1 a county pays in federal taxes, how much comes back? Pick any county at the top; every frame uses real fiscal 2023 numbers.

Why the adopted view feels overwhelming

  • No single focal point: the county page opens with 3 columns, 7 bars and 4 rank figures at once.
  • The answer is a number, not a sentence. "$0.75 back per $1 of federal tax" has to be decoded.
  • Everything has equal weight: the table, three sidebar boxes, notes and "est." tags all compete with the answer.
  • Comparisons are scattered: U.S. average, typical county, two ranks and neighbors sit in four places.
  • The home page shows a map but never asks the question; search is a small box in a corner.

Principles for the iterations

  • One question per screen, answered in a plain sentence.
  • One hero picture that shows "paid" next to "came back".
  • One comparison line instead of four scattered ranks.
  • Details on demand: tables, estimates and caveats one tap away, never removed.
  • A centered, narrow column (about 680px), like a well-set article.

1 · Answer first

The home page asks the question and puts a big search in the middle; the map becomes a secondary way in. The county page is one centered column: name, a one-sentence answer, one picture (what residents paid vs. what came back), one comparison line, then everything else in folded rows.

  • Inspired by: GOV.UK service pages (one thing per page), Our World in Data's "key insight first", the IRS "Where's My Refund" answer-first pattern.
  • Keeps: Atlas look and map, Ledger's paid vs. came back, Statement detail (folded), Explore (linked).
  • Drops from view: sidebars, the three-column ledger, ranks above the fold.

2 · Cents on the dollar

Everything is translated into one unit: cents back per $1 of federal tax. The county page draws the residents' $1 as a bill and the money that came back as a filled bill (or several bills, when more than $1 came back), split into Social Security, grants and contracts. Comparisons use the same little bills.

  • Inspired by: "where your tax dollar goes" pie charts in budget documents, the UK Annual Tax Summary's per-£ breakdown, Pew's per-dollar framing.
  • Keeps: Atlas map (legend in cents), Ledger's in vs. out as two bills, one Statement table on demand.
  • Drops: billions on the first screen. Totals move into the details.

County page

Better

One unit everywhere, so a county of 2,000 and a county of 2 million compare instantly. "26¢ of every dollar came back as Social Security" is easy to repeat and share.

Watch

Cents hide the size of the money, so totals and per-person dollars must stay one tap away. Counties far above $1 need the "×" label to stay readable.

3 · Step by step

The home page is the map, but tapping a county opens a small card with the answer right away. The county page is a short guided sequence: 1 the answer, 2 what came in, 3 what residents paid, 4 how it compares. One idea per step, with Next and Back. The full tables are the last step.

  • Inspired by: map apps' bottom sheets, step-by-step tax software (one question per screen), Pudding-style guided explainers.
  • Keeps: Atlas map, Ledger in two separate steps, Statement table as the final step, Explore linked from step 4.
  • Drops: the long scroll. Every step fits on one phone screen.
